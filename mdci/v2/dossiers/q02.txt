Domain and target estimand
The setting is schooling and labor-market earnings in the United States. The target is the effect of additional completed schooling on subsequent weekly earnings for people whose schooling duration can be changed by compulsory attendance requirements. A related target is the effect of those requirements on school enrollment and completed schooling. The population relevant to the earnings question is primarily United States-born male workers in specified birth cohorts; enrollment evidence also includes boys and girls.

Observed variables and evidence availability
Population census extracts report quarter of birth, age, completed schooling, wage and salary earnings, weeks worked, race, marital status, geographic characteristics, and other personal characteristics. Earnings samples use the 1970 and 1980 censuses, with cohorts born in the 1920s and later decades. Enrollment evidence uses the 1960, 1970, and 1980 censuses and children born in 1944, 1954, and 1964 respectively. A separate compilation records state compulsory attendance ages and legal qualifications. Weekly earnings are constructed from annual earnings and weeks worked. Some older census variables are categorical and require assigned interval values. Schooling is recorded as years completed. The available evidence consists of population snapshots and legal records, rather than a complete individual schooling and employment history.

Undirected dependencies
Candidate dependencies include schooling–earnings, birth quarter–age at school entry, birth quarter–age on a census date, attendance requirements–enrollment, cohort–earnings, location–schooling opportunities, and race–labor-market conditions. Family resources, preferences, underlying skills, health, and local school quality may connect schooling and earnings. Birth timing could also be associated with family characteristics or environmental conditions. These are plausible dependencies to consider before inspecting empirical results, without asserting their magnitude or direction.

Negotiable institutional and functional prior knowledge
Many districts condition entry on reaching a specified age by a calendar cutoff, while attendance laws set a minimum leaving age. The interaction can change the amount of schooling completed before leaving is legally permitted. Enforcement, exceptions, delayed entry, grade progression, and voluntary continuation are behavioral or institutional qualifications. Claims that birth season has no other earnings pathway, that family composition is invariant across birth quarters, or that schooling has a common earnings response are unverified assumptions. Legal requirements do not guarantee compliance.

Physically constrained directional knowledge
Date of birth, chronological age, birth quarter, and weekly earnings have arithmetic or definitional relations. These belong to logical and measurement constraints, not physical evidence for an education-to-earnings mechanism. No domain-specific physical law identifying the target schooling effect is supplied. Earlier occurrence alone does not rule out common causes of recorded associations.

Observation limitations
Earnings samples exclude people without positive wage and salary earnings or weeks worked and exclude specified census-allocated responses. Employment participation, income coverage, categorical reporting, and measurement error therefore matter. Actual entry dates, individual enforcement histories, family background, and skills are not comprehensively observed. State and regional measures do not capture every district rule. Different cohorts and census samples need not represent identical labor markets.
