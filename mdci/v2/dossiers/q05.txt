The domain is the market for differentiated durable consumer products. The target causal quantities describe how a change in a vehicle's price or attributes changes demand for that vehicle and its substitutes, together with the relationship between product attributes and production costs. These quantities support policy comparisons in which taxes, trade conditions, product availability, or other market conditions change the prices and purchases that consumers face. A relevant response can include households switching to another new vehicle or foregoing a new vehicle purchase. Both the response holding other prices fixed and the response when producers adjust their prices require explicit definition.

The evidence contains annual product-level observations on passenger vehicles sold in a national market. Available characteristics include engine and body dimensions, horsepower, weight, doors, cylinders, fuel economy, transmission, drive configuration, and standard equipment. Recorded prices are base-model list retail prices, converted to constant purchasing-power units; they are not household transaction prices. Sales are observed by vehicle nameplate. Product characteristics describe the base model even when nameplate sales include multiple variants. Products enter and leave the observed market. Very small specialty products are omitted. Product ownership is classified by parent company, so several brands may share one producer. A country-of-origin designation follows the producing firm's affiliation rather than the physical location of every assembly plant.

Additional evidence includes the number of households, annual household income distributions from a population survey, fuel prices, and a relative reliability index for part of the product set. Household income records are not linked to individual vehicle purchases. The reliability measure is incomplete and its scale changes across years. Fleet purchases are not separately observed. Consequently, individual substitution paths, negotiated discounts, and the costs of supplying particular models are not directly recorded in the main market data.

Prices, quantities, product quality, and production conditions can depend on one another. An appealing unrecorded feature may raise sales and affect a producer's pricing decision. Production costs can influence prices, while sales volume and product assortment may influence costs. Income and tastes affect the mix of purchases; manufacturers may respond to anticipated demand when selecting attributes. The observed association of price with sales does not by itself establish which of these dependencies dominates. A product's observed attributes need not be independent of omitted quality or production conditions.

Negotiable priors include purposive consumer choice, stable preferences over product features, firms seeking profits, common control of brands within a parent company, and a particular way producers react to competitors. Restrictions on substitution patterns, cost functions, available alternatives, and the stability of the relationship between measured and unmeasured attributes are also contestable. They should not be treated as physical constraints.

No nonnegotiable physical direction determines the response of aggregate purchases to a market intervention. Mechanical characteristics constrain feasible engineering configurations, but do not fix consumer valuation, monetary costs, or price-setting behavior. The principal limitations are product aggregation, lack of matched purchaser records, unobserved quality and costs, and the difference between list and transaction prices.
