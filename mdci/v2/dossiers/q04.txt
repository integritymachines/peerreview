The domain is public transport fleet maintenance and replacement investment. The target policy quantity is how changing the cost of replacing a worn engine would change the timing and frequency of replacements and the resulting demand for replacement investment. Related quantities concern the tradeoff between continuing operation, maintenance expenditures, unexpected failures, and paying for an overhaul. The decision unit is an individual bus engine operated within a common fleet management organization. The relevant intervention changes an economic cost faced by the maintenance decision maker rather than assigning a different odometer reading to an otherwise identical engine.

The available evidence consists of monthly odometer records and maintenance diaries from a municipal bus operator. Diaries record dates, mileage, and components repaired or replaced when a bus visits the workshop. These records distinguish routine periodic maintenance, component repairs following failure, and major engine overhaul or replacement. Fleet descriptors include manufacturer, engine type, purchase vintage, seating capacity, vehicle weight, purchase price, and an assessed vehicle value. Some vehicles have a recorded replacement and others remain in operation without a replacement during observation. Observation begins at an administrative start date or a later purchase date and ends at a common administrative endpoint. The records therefore contain incomplete operating spells as well as spells ending in replacement. Comparisons can be made within engines over time and among vehicles with different histories, but replacement costs and the full economic costs of operating each engine are not directly measured as a complete contemporaneous ledger.

Mileage, age, operating intensity, mechanical condition, repair history, and replacement decisions can be dependent. Continued use raises recorded accumulated mileage; replacement changes which engine supplies subsequent service. Neither chronological ordering nor a mileage record alone establishes how much deterioration mileage causes, how much unrecorded condition affects replacement, or how managers alter vehicle use in anticipation of workshop activity. Differences across vehicle groups can reflect procurement, construction, assignment, or operating conditions. A manager may observe symptoms absent from the retained records.

Negotiable priors include increasing expected maintenance burdens with wear, comparability of rebuilt and new engines, a common decision objective across vehicles, and attention to future costs when choosing current maintenance. Whether the manager minimizes expected discounted expenditures, how future service is valued, and whether mileage adequately summarizes economically relevant deterioration are behavioral and functional assumptions. They are not physical laws. The absence of a newly observed repair is not evidence of unchanged latent condition.

An engine replacement physically changes the installed hardware and resets the identity of the operating engine. Distance traveled is an accumulated record of movement, not a free-standing economic cause. No nonnegotiable physical law fixes a monotone relation between mileage and monetary maintenance expenditure, or dictates the manager's response to a replacement price. Generalization is limited by the common organization, incomplete condition measurement, administrative observation boundaries, and the difference between observed historical choices and responses to a changed cost environment.
