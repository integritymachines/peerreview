The domain is unemployment insurance, household liquidity, and job search. The target is a policy-relevant decomposition of the effect of unemployment benefits on unemployment duration into a response to increased resources available during unemployment and a response to altered financial incentives for returning to work. Related causal quantities are the duration response to an unconditional cash payment and how these responses differ with access to liquid resources. The policy objective concerns the welfare consequences of changing benefit generosity, taking account of resources transferred to unemployed households, financing costs, and induced changes in the length of benefit receipt. This is a question about behavioral responses and policy valuation rather than a comparison of observed unemployment rates alone.

The evidence includes household panel surveys recording weekly employment status, unemployment benefit receipt, earnings histories, demographics, and asset holdings. The retained unemployment spells concern prime-age men searching for work who are not on temporary layoff, have a recorded employment history, and begin claiming benefits shortly after job loss. Assets are collected less frequently than employment histories, so an asset measurement may precede or follow the unemployment spell. Liquid wealth can be distinguished from housing and other less readily accessible resources. Mortgage and household circumstances provide additional information about potential expenditure commitments.

Benefit rules differ across states and calendar years. Administrative summaries and state legal provisions describe maximum payments and the relationship between prior wages and benefits. Individual benefit entitlement is imperfectly observed. Available measures therefore include state-level average payments, statutory maxima, and payments calculated by applying benefit rules to predicted previous earnings. These are distinct measurements rather than interchangeable records of actual household cash receipts.

Separate surveys of displaced workers, linked to state unemployment insurance records, include reported unemployment spells, severance receipt, prior wages, job tenure, occupation, industry, demographics, and household income. One survey oversamples people who exhaust benefits; sampling weights describe how to recover the intended worker population. Temporary layoffs, missing required variables, and demographic restrictions affect the retained sample. Receipt of severance is not assigned at random. Its precise payment schedule is unavailable, and employer survey information supplies context about how payments relate to tenure. The target comparison concerns cash resources rather than any other treatment that may have existed in a survey's parent program.

Several directions remain unsettled. Wealth can affect search choices while earlier earnings and employment choices affect wealth. Job characteristics, employer policies, tenure, and worker characteristics can jointly affect severance and subsequent unemployment. Benefit rules can be related to local labor market conditions. Longer unemployment can itself deplete assets. Search intensity, job offers, acceptance decisions, and available borrowing are not completely observed.

Negotiable priors include the valuation of consumption during unemployment, the way cash availability constrains choices, the response of search to financial incentives, and the stability of job opportunities when benefits change. Treating severance as an unconditional cash transfer, transporting responses across worker groups, and translating duration responses into welfare also require contestable assumptions. No physical law determines the sign of a benefit or wealth effect on job search. Administrative payment rules constrain eligibility and budgets institutionally; they do not establish a compulsory behavioral direction. Limits include selected claimants, asset timing, imperfect entitlement measurement, nonrandom severance, and incomplete measurement of job quality and consumption.
