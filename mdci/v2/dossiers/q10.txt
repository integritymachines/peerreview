The domain is climate exposure and national economic development. The target causal quantity is the change in economic output and its growth path caused by a change in temperature, with precipitation also considered as an environmental exposure. The question distinguishes an effect that changes output temporarily from one that changes subsequent growth and therefore compounds over time. Effects may differ across countries with different initial incomes, climates, agricultural dependence, or institutions. Extending a response to short-lived weather variation into a response to sustained warming is a separate target requiring assumptions about persistence and adjustment.

The evidence combines repeated national economic accounts with geographically detailed weather records. Monthly temperature and precipitation fields are constructed from weather stations using spatial interpolation and elevation corrections. These fields are aggregated to annual country exposures. Population weights come from a fixed spatial population distribution; geographic-area averages are also available. National output and population measures allow output per person and its annual growth to be constructed. An alternative international economic accounts source is available for comparison. The main country coverage requires a sufficiently long observed output history, so data availability determines inclusion rather than representing all possible economies equally.

Further evidence includes agricultural and industrial activity, investment, political events and institutions, and country characteristics describing development and baseline environmental conditions. Some measures cover only subsets of countries or years. The principal records measure aggregate production and annual exposure rather than worker-level heat, household adaptation expenditure, or the physical conditions of individual farms and factories. A country's mean exposure can hide substantial geographic variation. Population weighting emphasizes places where people were located in the reference distribution; it does not continuously update settlement patterns as they respond to economic change.

Temperature, rainfall, production, investment, and political stability may be connected through several routes. Weather can influence crop conditions, work capacity, infrastructure, disease environments, conflict, and resource allocation. Output changes can alter investment and institutions, which can alter later output. Long-run development can affect settlement, measurement capacity, emissions, and adaptation. A sequence in which weather is recorded before an economic outcome does not alone determine all the links among these variables. A national output response cannot automatically be assigned to agriculture, labor productivity, or political disruption without further evidence.

Negotiable priors concern the persistence of production responses, the comparability of exposure across climates, adaptation to expected conditions, the role of initial income, and whether channels add or interact. Stability of borders, accounts definitions, and the meaning of annual country averages also matter. These are empirical or institutional assumptions, not physical laws. A growth response need not have the same sign or duration as an immediate production response.

Temperature and precipitation describe physical environmental states. Physical heat transfer and biological exposure impose real constraints locally, but they do not establish a universal directional law from national mean temperature to monetary output or political behavior. The dossier therefore supplies no compulsory signed economic effect. Limitations include interpolated weather, exposure aggregation, selective accounts coverage, unmeasured adaptation, overlapping channels, and the uncertain transport of historically observed weather responses to persistent climate change.
