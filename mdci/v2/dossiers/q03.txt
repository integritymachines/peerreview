Domain and target estimand
The setting is employment in chain fast-food restaurants in two neighboring United States states. The target is the effect of an increase in the legal hourly wage floor on restaurant employment over the ensuing months. Store employment, hours of operation, prices, and compensation practices are distinct possible responses. The immediate population is the sampled restaurant chains in the covered region, rather than every business or every worker.

Observed variables and evidence availability
One state's hourly minimum rose from $4.25 to $5.05 on April 1, 1992; the neighboring state's minimum remained at $4.25. Managers were surveyed shortly before the increase, in February and March, and again in November and December. The initial sampling frame consisted of restaurants with working telephone numbers from four chains. There were 473 reachable stores and 410 initial interviews. The follow-up attempted to revisit those initial respondents; stores unreachable by telephone could receive personal visits. Recorded quantities include full-time and part-time employees, managers, starting wages, hours open, meal prices, ownership or franchise status, chain, and location. Information on temporary and permanent closure is relevant to defining employment outcomes. Different weighting of full-time, part-time, and management staff produces different measures of labor input. Stores also differed in their initial wage rates and hence in the legal adjustment required.

Undirected dependencies
The prior dependency inventory includes wages–employment, customer demand–staffing, prices–sales, hours open–labor requirements, chain–business practices, location–demand, and ownership–compensation policy. Local economic conditions may connect the wage legislation, restaurant demand, and employment. Initial wages, recruitment difficulty, staffing composition, and store productivity may be related. Neighboring labor and product markets may connect stores across the state boundary. These are candidate dependencies and do not state observed relationships.

Negotiable institutional and functional prior knowledge
The effective date, statutory wage rates, surveyed chains, and sampling procedures are documented. Coverage and compliance determine how a legal floor becomes a wage actually paid. Employers may adjust staffing, prices, hours, benefits, recruiting, or production methods. Profit seeking alone does not fix the sign of an employment response. Similar trends across locations, absence of cross-border adjustment, and a stable mapping from reported headcounts to hours are assumptions requiring separate support. Common seasonal patterns or shared chain policies may matter, but their equality across stores is not guaranteed.

Physically constrained directional knowledge
A legal threshold and the arithmetic conversion of counts to full-time-equivalent staffing are institutional and definitional constraints, not physical laws. Neither establishes a physically necessary employer response. No domain-specific physical constraint identifying the target employment effect is supplied. Employment, pay, demand, and prices can interact; their orientations are not fixed by the survey calendar.

Observation limitations
Manager reports are not payroll records or complete worker-hour histories. Initial nonresponse and follow-up availability can affect coverage. Closed stores require explicit treatment in outcome construction. Chain selection, regional coverage, and the short follow-up restrict generalization. Unmeasured demand, worker turnover, and changes in productivity or benefits can remain relevant.
