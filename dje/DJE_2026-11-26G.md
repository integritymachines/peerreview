### 1. Raymond, Dillon — AI-Assisted Programming and Labor Demand (arXiv 2611.31083v1 [econ.GN], submitted 23 Nov 2026; paper dated 6 Nov 2026)
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31083
**Question:** Does firm adoption of an AI coding assistant (GitHub Copilot) change how many and which technical workers firms hire?
**Method:**
- Data: GitHub Enterprise sales/CRM and Copilot adoption records for 5,414 multinational firms (the authors say 38% of the US programming workforce), linked to Revelio Labs (mainly LinkedIn-based) firm-level headcount, hires, separations and postings.
- Design: instrumented, staggered difference-in-differences. The instrument is each firm's predetermined GitHub Enterprise contract-review date. Reduced forms use the Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess imputation estimator with firm and calendar-month fixed effects and firm-clustered SEs.
- First stage: a scheduled review raises Copilot adoption by 8.6 pp after 6 months and 19.3 pp after 12 months (end-of-sample adoption 62%); effective F = 30.7 at four quarters.
- Checks: placebo review dates before Copilot's 2023 launch; accountants' hiring as a non-technical placebo; size-tercile and sector × quarter trends; role-by-seniority cells, with coding intensity measured from pre-launch LinkedIn job descriptions.
**Result:**
- Reduced form, 12 months after review: log technical hires −0.079 (SE 0.038), on firm-months with positive hires. Separations +0.029 (0.041) and log technical headcount −0.016 (0.018) are not significant.
- Wald estimate (Q4): −0.340 log points (≈ −29%) in quarterly technical hiring among compliers. The 95% firm-block bootstrap CI is [−0.948, 0.088], so the instrumented effect is not statistically distinguishable from zero.
- Postings fall 14% (intensive margin); vacancy duration does not change. That points to lower demand, not slower matching.
- Composition: neither code intensity alone (−0.023, SE 0.022) nor seniority alone (−0.013, SE 0.040) predicts the decline. Their interaction does: junior hiring falls 0.071 log points more per SD of code intensity. Among compliers, junior software-engineer hiring falls about 40%, and the code-intensive share of junior hires drops from 72% to an implied 52%. Experienced hires get more experienced (+1.3 years) and are more often postgraduates (+4.7 pp); external experienced hires fall about 13%.
**Beyond prior work:** Task-level RCTs (e.g. Copilot speed-ups, call-centre AI) find the biggest productivity gains for novices. Posting- and payroll-based studies of AI exposure lack firm adoption data. This paper ties actual firm adoption to hiring, using quasi-random timing from procurement cycles, and finds labor demand moving against the very group with the largest task-level gains.
**Surviving claim:** In large incumbent software employers, Copilot adoption slowed technical hiring (reduced form) without layoffs or headcount change within one year. The slowdown is concentrated in junior, code-intensive roles, and the experienced hiring bar went up.
**Decisive limitations:**
- The 29% headline is the Wald point estimate; its CI includes zero. The reduced form (≈ −8%) is the firmer number.
- The key heterogeneity is a role × seniority interaction that appears only after both main effects come out null. Multiplicity across 16 cells and specifications is not adjusted.
- Revelio/LinkedIn measures hiring with lags and selective coverage. The authors flag that LinkedIn position text can be edited after the fact.
- Conflict of interest: Copilot is a Microsoft product, one author is at Microsoft Research, and the adoption data are proprietary GitHub records. This is disclosed. Replication outside Microsoft is not possible from the paper alone.
- The one-year horizon and the incumbent-firm sample limit transport to entrants and to the aggregate market.
**Why selected:** It is the first credible firm-level causal design linking actual generative-AI adoption to hiring at scale. The headline policy question (are entry-level jobs the margin?) gets a specific, falsifiable answer, with honest reporting of a wide IV interval.

### 2. Amorim Cabaço, Ravn — Antitrust, Market Power, and U.S. Economic Outcomes (CEPR DP 22186, released 16 Nov 2026; author version dated 7 Nov 2026)
Link: https://cepr.org/publications/dp22186 (full text read from the author's copy: https://brian-amorim-cabaco.github.io/AmorimCabacoRavnAntitrust.pdf)
**Question:** What has federal antitrust enforcement done to markups, output, inputs and innovation in the US since the 1950s?
**Method:**
- New narrative database of all DoJ and FTC antitrust filings since the mid-1950s: 18,805 defendant observations, each case checked at least three times. Cases are classified as civil non-merger, criminal or civil merger, with outcomes.
- Firm level: synthetic difference-in-differences on Compustat defendants in non-merger and criminal cases (134 defendants against 1,789 never-treated controls). Markups come from production functions (De Loecker–Warzynski, COGS-based).
- Sector level: enforcement proxies built from defendants' cumulative abnormal returns around filings, weighted by market capitalization. Panel local projections cover 19 BEA sectors (about 1957–2024) with sector and time fixed effects, bias correction and Driscoll–Kraay SEs. The proxies then instrument for markups. A panel VAR provides counterfactuals.
**Result:**
- Composition shift: merger cases go from 21.9% of listed corporate defendant observations (1957–80) to 75.6% (1981–2025). Documented relief is obtained in more than 90% of cases with known outcomes.
- Defendants (non-merger and criminal): markups −2.8% after 5 years; real sales +12.9%; real COGS +16.7%; employment +7.6% (not significant).
- Equity markets: CARs one month after filing are about −1.9% for merger cases and about −1% for non-merger and criminal cases. Post-1980, the non-merger/criminal responses are no longer statistically distinguishable from zero.
- Sectors: more enforcement lowers markups, concentration and profit margins (mostly in the upper tail of markups) and raises value added, R&D capital and patents. Merger enforcement raises output without sustained input increases.
- IV: a 1% markup increase lowers real value added by about 1.4–1.9% after 5 years. Counterfactual: without enforcement, aggregate markups would be about 2.2 pp (non-merger and criminal) and 4.1 pp (merger) higher by 2024.
**Beyond prior work:** The markup-trend literature (De Loecker–Eeckhout–Unger; Döpper et al.) documents rising market power, and case studies and merger retrospectives assess single cases. This paper offers the first economy-wide, seven-decade evidence on the macro and sector effects of enforcement, using a new public-records dataset and a market-based measure of case importance.
**Surviving claim:** The new filing record and the enforcement-composition facts are a durable contribution. Defendant-level evidence that non-merger and criminal cases lower markups while expanding output is credible. The sector-level magnitudes are suggestive.
**Decisive limitations:**
- With COGS-based markups, a rise in COGS relative to sales lowers measured markups mechanically for a given output elasticity. The firm-level "markup fall" and "input expansion" are therefore not independent findings.
- The sector proxy relies on stock-market surprises. Enforcement is partly predictable (concentration predicts it; R² ≈ 4–7%). Post-1980 non-merger CARs are statistically null, which weakens the proxy exactly where the composition shift happens.
- The authors themselves argue that merger enforcement raises output through productivity and allocation. That is a channel other than markups, which undercuts the exclusion restriction behind the markup IV elasticity (1.4–1.9).
- Inference rests on 19 sectors (Driscoll–Kraay). The VAR counterfactuals add model dependence.
**Why selected:** It addresses a first-order policy question with a new, likely widely used dataset and multiple layers of evidence. The firm-level and descriptive results are strong even if the macro elasticities are taken as provisional.

### 3. Hansen, Lippi, Sargent — Behavioral Bias, Relative to What? Robustness and Discounting in a Bewley Economy (CEPR DP 22184, Nov 2026; author version dated 15 Oct 2026)
Link: https://cepr.org/publications/dp22184 (full text read from https://larspeterhansen.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/lq_robust_bewley.pdf)
**Question:** Can quantity data tell a less patient consumer apart from one who fears model misspecification? If not, what does it mean to call behavior "biased"?
**Method:** Theory. A linear-quadratic Bewley ("borrowers and lenders") economy in which consumers differ in discount factor β̂ and in Hansen–Sargent multiplier-robustness σ. Closed-form scalar Riccati solutions; an observational-equivalence locus; detection-error probabilities; a numerical illustration (β = 0.95, annual).
**Result:**
- Proposition 3: a one-dimensional locus of (σ, β̂) on which every type's marginal propensity to consume equals the trusting benchmark's (1 − β).
- Proposition 4: any distribution of types on the locus supports R = β⁻¹ and the same joint distribution of consumption, debt and income. Patience and robustness are not separately identified from quantities, even with the endowment law known.
- Types differ in worst-case forecasts, shadow prices and welfare. All satisfy an Euler-equation restriction that can be tested with elicited discount factors and forecasts from the same people.
- Bounds: robustness can make a consumer look at most about twice as impatient. In the example, admissible rates of time preference run from 5.3% to just under 10.8% per year. Detection-error probability: above 0.43 at T = 10; at T = 40 about 0.199 at the breakdown point; at T = 160 it hits 0.2 about a quarter of the way to that point.
- Measured from the benchmark, a robust consumer looks pessimistic and impatient. Measured from another type on the locus, he can look optimistic and patient.
**Beyond prior work:** Earlier observational-equivalence results (Hansen–Sargent–Tallarini 1999; Hansen–Sargent 2008) restrict one agent's robustness parameter. Luo et al. (2020) put a common robustness concern into a Huggett economy, which moves the interest rate. Here a whole distribution of heterogeneous types is equilibrium-invariant, which turns an identification point into a critique of reference-model choice in behavioral attributions.
**Surviving claim:** In this LQ class, quantity data cannot pick the reference model needed to label patience or belief "biases"; elicited beliefs and discount factors are required. The bounds and detection errors make that freedom finite and quantifiable.
**Decisive limitations:**
- Exact invariance relies on the LQ structure (certainty equivalence and closed-form aggregation). The authors note there is no general approximate-aggregation theorem (Remark 3), so transport to CRRA or borrowing-constrained Bewley models is open.
- Types are placed on the locus by assumption.
- The welfare and planner questions (whose worst case counts) are explicitly left open.
**Why selected:** It is a sharp, general-interest conceptual contribution at the intersection of macro heterogeneity, robustness and behavioral economics. It changes what empirical behavioral claims must condition on, and it offers a concrete test with elicited data.

### 4. Aga, Jolevski — First Impressions Matter: Measuring and Correcting Position Bias in Business Environment Estimates (World Bank Policy Research WP 11506, Nov 2026)
Link: https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/11506.html (PDF via documents.worldbank.org)
**Question:** How much does the order of response options distort the "biggest obstacle" question in the World Bank Enterprise Surveys, a key input to development policy diagnostics?
**Method:**
- More than 200,000 manager interviews from 178 economies, using built-in experimental variation. Before 2017 there were three rotated showcard versions assigned at the sampling stage; since 2017 the order of 15 options is fully randomized and recorded in the CAPI script. Surveys with unbalanced rotation or unrecorded orders are excluded.
- Selection-ratio heatmaps and choice models with survey and respondent controls.
- Empirical-Bayes-stabilized inverse-probability weights correct the bias.
**Result:**
- Options in the first three positions are about 9% more likely to be chosen; the first position about 22% (regression estimate; the descriptive mean selection ratio for position 1 is 17%).
- Primacy bias is about 17% in low-income economies and statistically absent in high-income economies.
- The correction changes the rank order for 9% of obstacles and affects about half of the surveys.
**Beyond prior work:** Response-order (primacy) effects are long documented in survey methodology (Krosnick–Alwin tradition). The contribution is a large randomized measurement across economies on a policy-critical instrument, plus a deployable correction.
**Surviving claim:** Position effects are real, are largest where measurement matters most (low-income settings), and are big enough to reorder constraint rankings in many country surveys.
**Decisive limitations:**
- In the pre-2017 rotation subsample, position is crossed with only three fixed orders.
- The headline first-position number differs across estimators (22% vs 17%).
- The correction assumes position effects are separable from content and homogeneous within strata; validation against an external benchmark of "true" constraints is not possible.
- Significance is narrower than for the other selections: measurement for one survey program, although a widely used one.
**Why selected:** It is a clean, randomized, very large-scale measurement result with direct consequences for a widely cited policy dataset. It is the strongest empirical-measurement paper in this window. Selected as the fourth paper, ahead of abstract-only contenders whose full text was not accessible.

- *Deeper check, inference:*
  - Province-level randomization inference has only C(9,6) = 84 possible assignments, so the smallest attainable RI p-value is 1/84 ≈ 0.012 (stated in the Table S2 notes). Design-based inference therefore cannot deliver strong rejections for any single outcome. The wild-cluster bootstrap (Webb weights, 9 clusters) carries the weight.
  - Balance on 16 baseline outcomes is not rejected (RI p = 0.50).
- *Deeper check, deaths:*
  - In the first window (Feb–Apr 2025), recorded ART deaths *fall* by 65 per month. The authors attribute this to under-reporting and treat the final window as the most reliable: 81 per month [24, 139]; 486 [142, 837] over the last six months.
  - The 15-month total is 625 [−266, 1,665]. Across the four Lusaka/North-Western sample permutations (Table S10), the figure reaches up to 963 [−134, 2,235].
  - The roughly 1,800 figure (adjusting for unregistered deaths) multiplies by a tracing-study ratio (Holmes et al.). It is an extrapolation, not an estimate.
- *Assessment:*
  - The service-disruption results (testing −22%, PrEP −44%, loss to follow-up +53%) are robust and well identified.
  - The downstream infection results (HIV-positive pregnancies +14% [3, 25]) are credible with plausible lags.
  - The mortality headline is the most fragile link: window choice after an unexplained early dip, few clusters, no multiplicity adjustment. It should be read as "recorded ART deaths rose substantially by late 2025", not as a precise toll.

## #S — Scorecards
Severity: fatal/critical/fixable. Visibility: obvious/tricky/hidden. Color shows repair effort: 🟢 none, 🟡 local, 🟠 substantial, 🔴 fundamental.

### arXiv 2611.31083 Raymond, Dillon
**Traditions:** labor economics of technology and tasks (Autor–Levy–Murnane; Acemoglu–Restrepo); design-based econometrics (Imbens–Angrist LATE; Borusyak–Jaravel–Spiess); personnel and organizational economics (Garicano–Rossi-Hansberg).
**Summary:** Using predetermined GitHub contract-review dates as an instrument for Copilot adoption across 5,414 large firms, the paper finds that technical hiring slows (reduced form ≈ −8% at 12 months) with no change in separations or headcount. The decline is concentrated in junior, code-intensive roles, while experienced hiring becomes more selective.
**Flags:**
- **Calibration / inference (ME5): 🟡 fixable / obvious / verified.** The Q4 Wald estimate of −29% has a bootstrap CI of [−0.948, 0.088] log points (§6.1.1). Abstract-level magnitudes overstate precision. Repair: lead with the reduced form, or pool horizons.
- **Statistical / multiplicity (ME8): 🟡 fixable / tricky / suspicion.** Neither main effect is significant. The junior × code-intensity interaction (−0.086), and its echo across 16 cells, is the headline heterogeneity and is unadjusted. Defense: a consistent sign pattern across all eight junior cells. Repair: pre-specified tests or sample splits.
- **Causal / timing (ME3): 🟡 / tricky / unresolved.** First-stage leads slope upward. The composition explanation is plausible but leaves anticipation untestable. Defense: pre-launch placebo reviews show no hiring effect.
- **Measurement (Survey): 🟡 / hidden / suspicion.** Revelio/LinkedIn hiring counts and ex-post-editable job text. Bias is likely non-differential by review date.
- **Integrity / conflict: 🟡 / obvious / disclosed.** Microsoft-owned product and data, plus a Microsoft-affiliated coauthor. Nothing suggests manipulation, but external replication is infeasible.
- **Precedence: 🟢.**
**Strongest surviving claim:** adoption timing driven by procurement cycles reduces hiring flows, not stocks, within a year, tilted against junior code-intensive roles.

### CEPR 22186 Amorim Cabaço, Ravn
**Traditions:** empirical IO and antitrust (Bresnahan; Baker; Kwoka merger retrospectives); production-function markups (Hall; De Loecker–Warzynski); macro-econometric local projections and narrative identification (Jordà; Romer–Romer).
**Summary:** A new seven-decade record of DoJ and FTC filings shows the shift to merger enforcement after 1980. Defendant- and sector-level evidence indicates that enforcement lowers markups and raises output, R&D and patents; without enforcement, aggregate markups would be 2.2–4.1 pp higher.
**Flags:**
- **Measurement (MI2/ME1): 🟠 critical-for-mechanism / tricky / verified.** Markups are output elasticity × sales/COGS. COGS rising faster than sales (+16.7% vs +12.9%) is the markup decline, so the "input expansion" and "markup fall" are one fact. Repair: demand-side or alternative-input markups (the Cobb–Douglas variant does not address this).
- **Identification (ME3/ME9): 🟠 / tricky / verified.** The proxies are generated regressors built from CARs. Post-1980 non-merger CARs are null, and enforcement is partly predictable (R² ≈ 4–7%). Defense: most variation is unpredictable after fixed effects; there are many robustness checks.
- **IV exclusion (ME3): 🟠 / hidden / verified.** The paper's own interpretation that merger enforcement works through productivity contradicts the exclusion restriction for markups as the sole channel, so the 1.4–1.9 elasticity is not a structural markup effect. Repair: reframe as a reduced form.
- **Inference (ME5): 🟡 / obvious.** Only 19 sectors (Driscoll–Kraay).
- **Source (narrative record): 🟢.** Triple-checked case coding; outcomes known for 79–91% of filings.
**Strongest surviving claim:** the dataset and composition facts; firm-level non-merger and criminal filings precede lower measured markups and higher sales.

### CEPR 22184 Hansen, Lippi, Sargent
**Traditions:** recursive macro and heterogeneous agents (Bewley; Huggett; Ljungqvist–Sargent); decision theory under ambiguity (Gilboa–Schmeidler; Maccheroni–Marinacci–Rustichini); behavioral economics (Laibson; Bernheim–Rangel on welfare with biased agents).
**Summary:** In an LQ Bewley economy, an explicit locus of (patience, robustness) pairs gives identical consumption-saving rules. Any population on the locus yields the same interest rate and quantity distributions, so quantity data cannot locate the reference model behind "bias" attributions. Bounds (at most about a doubling of apparent impatience) and detection-error probabilities discipline the freedom.
**Flags:**
- **Formal / scope (Formal; GE4): 🟡 fixable / obvious / acknowledged.** Exact invariance uses LQ certainty equivalence. Generalization to borrowing constraints or CRRA preferences is open (Remark 3). The claim is stated within the class.
- **Assumption (MI1): 🟡 / tricky.** Types are assumed to lie on the locus; off-locus populations break invariance, and the paper bounds rather than tests this.
- **Normative (Normative): 🟢 / acknowledged.** Planner and worst-case aggregation are left open (fn.).
- **Proofs:** not checked; the appendix proofs were not read.
**Strongest surviving claim:** behavioral-bias attributions from quantity data alone are reference-model-dependent; the paper's elicited-data Euler restriction is the discriminating test.

### WB WPS 11506 Aga, Jolevski
**Traditions:** survey methodology (Groves et al.; Krosnick–Alwin on response order); development measurement (Deaton); empirical-Bayes shrinkage (Efron–Morris; Robbins).
**Summary:** Randomized showcard orders across more than 200,000 Enterprise Survey interviews show primacy bias in the "biggest obstacle" question: 22% for the first position and about 17% in low-income economies. Correcting for it reorders 9% of obstacle ranks in about half of the surveys.
**Flags:**
- **Design (Experimental): 🟡 / tricky / verified.** Pre-2017 data have three fixed rotations, so position is only partially crossed with items. Defense: the post-2017 fully randomized subsample shows similar patterns (Table A3).
- **Consistency (Calibration): 🟡 / obvious / verified.** The first-position bias is 22% (model) vs 17% (mean ratio). Both are reported, but the abstract uses the larger figure.
- **Correction validity (Survey): 🟡 / hidden / unresolved.** IPW assumes separable, stratum-homogeneous position effects; there is no external ground truth.
- **Significance:** moderate; narrower scope than the other selections.
**Strongest surviving claim:** position effects in the Enterprise Surveys are material and concentrated in low-income settings.
