### 1. Asker, Collard-Wexler, De Cannière, De Loecker — Beyond the First Best: Welfare and OPEC Market Power (NBER WP 36058, v1, November 2026)
Link: https://www.nber.org/papers/w36058
**Question:** How much welfare has the OPEC cartel cost since 1970 once you allow for other distortions in the oil market? And how much of the cartel's power depends on those distortions?
**Method:**
- Data: Rystad field-level cost, production and reserves for tens of thousands of oil fields, 1970–2021, in 2021 USD.
- Approach: compare the observed allocation with simulated no-cartel market outcomes (dynamic Hotelling extraction with a choke price). The paper does not model how the cartel operates.
- Three concepts: total welfare loss (TWL, a naive measure that attributes every deviation from the planner to OPEC); marginal welfare loss (MWL, the cartel's incremental effect holding other distortions fixed); and inframarginal welfare loss (IWL, the cartel's effect once non-cartel cost-minimization violations are removed). MWL and IWL are each nested in TWL but do not add up to it, which is the second-best point.
- Demand: linear demand estimated by IV with 51 annual observations (price coefficient −69.66, SE 12.22). The baseline elasticity is −0.2 and yearly elasticities range from −0.31 to −0.05.
**Result:**
- TWL is about $2.2tn NPV since 1970: about $0.7tn deadweight loss and $1.5tn productive inefficiency.
- MWL is about $2.1tn, with DWL at 32%.
- IWL is about $0.7tn, of which only about 1% ($6bn) is DWL.
- Robustness (Table 4): TWL ranges from $1.85tn to $2.64tn and IWL from $0.57tn to $0.77tn across discount factors, choke prices, demand elasticities (−0.05 to −0.4), a flow constraint and reserve definitions. DWL scales with the elasticity ($0.37tn to $1.03tn).
- Counterfactual: if the US, Canada and the UK doubled output, the price would move substantially towards the competitive level.
**Beyond prior work:** The authors' earlier AER (2019) paper measured OPEC-induced productive misallocation. This paper adds a second-best welfare decomposition that separates cartel power from complementary frictions (fringe supply restrictions, regulation, non-cost-minimizing fringe producers). It is a dynamic Hotelling treatment with micro costs rather than a Stackelberg conduct model (Bornstein et al. 2023).
**Surviving claim:** OPEC's measured welfare cost is very large by IO standards. Most of it is productive inefficiency rather than deadweight loss. About two-thirds of the naive loss disappears if non-cartel producers minimized costs, so frictions outside OPEC complement its market power.
**Decisive limitations:**
- No statistical uncertainty is reported for the welfare totals, only parameter sensitivity.
- Carbon externalities are explicitly excluded and left to a companion paper. The welfare sign of the cartel is therefore not established.
- "Productive inefficiency" rests on Rystad cost estimates matching the costs firms actually expect to bear.
- Attributing the deviation from the planner to the cartel is a before-and-after comparison over 50 years (the authors' footnote 33 says as much).
**Why selected:** It makes a conceptual contribution (marginal versus inframarginal market-power loss under the second best), applies it with unique micro data to one of the largest markets in the world, and reaches a policy-relevant conclusion: expanding fringe supply substitutes for confronting the cartel directly.

### 2. Walter, Karlan, Moneke, Radu, Zyambo — Mortality Effects of the 2025 US Aid Freeze: Evidence from Zambia (NBER WP 36054, v1, November 2026)
Link: https://www.nber.org/papers/w36054
**Question:** What did the January 2025 freeze of US foreign aid actually do to HIV prevention, treatment, infections and deaths, as opposed to what models predicted?
**Method:**
- Design: difference-in-differences exploiting PEPFAR's geographic split in Zambia. USAID supported six northern provinces (hit hard; services disrupted for months), while CDC supported the southern provinces (resumed within weeks of the 1 Feb waiver).
- Data: monthly government HMIS data for the universe of Zambian health facilities (about 1,999 facilities in USAID provinces and 1,295 in CDC provinces), Nov 2023–Apr 2026, aggregated into three-month blocks with post-periods of 3/6/6 months.
- Inference: wild cluster bootstrap by province (9 clusters with Lusaka dropped; Webb weights; 9,999 reps), plus randomization inference over provinces for joint tests.
- Checks: pre-trend joint tests; differential non-reporting (entire missing form sections); placebo outcomes (Penta-3 vaccination, road accidents); sample variants (Lusaka in the controls, North-Western excluded, balanced panel).
**Result:**
- Immediately after the freeze, HIV tests fell by 173,000 (−22%) and PrEP initiations by 44%. Late pick-ups rose 57% and loss to follow-up 53%. Testing and PrEP recovered by about April 2025, but community outreach stayed about 51% lower.
- Later effects: HIV-positive pregnant women +14% [95% CI 3, 25] at 10–15 months; HIV-exposed births +15%; advanced-HIV ART starts +64% (10% level); TB +21%; meningitis +73–118%.
- Deaths among ART patients rose 26%, averaging 81/month [24, 139] in Nov 2025–Apr 2026. Over 15 months the total is 625 excess deaths [−266, 1,665]. These are recorded deaths only; the authors cite evidence that true mortality among patients lost to follow-up is higher.
- The estimate is a lower bound on the total freeze effect because the CDC comparison provinces were also briefly disrupted.
**Beyond prior work:** These are the first realized, population-wide causal estimates of the PEPFAR disruption. Earlier evidence came from ex-ante models or self-selected facility samples and PEPFAR's own disrupted reporting. The paper also adds the stability of donor engagement as a margin in the aid-modality debate.
**Surviving claim:** The freeze caused large short-run service disruptions in USAID-supported provinces. These were followed, with plausible biological lags, by more HIV-positive pregnancies, opportunistic infections and recorded ART-patient deaths.
**Decisive limitations:**
- There are only 9 province clusters (6 treated), so inference rests on the wild bootstrap with few clusters.
- Twelve outcomes and several windows are reported without multiplicity adjustment.
- The death headline comes from the final window; the 15-month cumulative CI includes zero, and the authors attribute the early dip in deaths to under-reporting.
- USAID also paid facility staff, so treatment can affect recording itself. The authors test for this, but it remains the main residual threat.
**Why selected:** It is the most policy-consequential empirical paper in the window: credible quasi-experimental evidence on the realized health cost of a major aid policy decision, with transparent inference and reporting checks.

### 3. Hoces de la Guardia, Miguel, Pathan, Shaji, Silva da Rocha, Sørensen, Tungodden — Beyond Publication Bias: Characterizing and Understanding Missing Results in Economics (NBER WP 36044, v1, November 2026)
Link: https://www.nber.org/papers/w36044 (pre-registered as AEARCTR-0013755)
**Question:** How many pre-registered hypotheses in economics never get publicly reported results? Is that driven by suppression of nulls? Can low-cost interventions recover missing results?
**Method:**
- Sample: 317 genuinely pre-registered RCTs from the AEA RCT Registry (Jan 2015–May 2017), coded into 5,057 hypotheses at about 11 hours of encoding per study. Searches were run 8–9 years after registration.
- Experiment: a pre-registered RCT with four arms (Control; Awareness email with an empty Results Report; Engagement with a pre-filled study-specific report; Resources, which adds up to 30 RA hours or $1,500). Availability was re-searched about 6 months later.
- Benchmark: forecasts from 68 experts.
**Result:**
- 80.8% of studies have some public output, but only 42% of hypotheses have available results (24.0% under a strict definition).
- 64.5% of available results are nulls at the 5% level. Experts underestimated both availability and the null share.
- Engagement and Resources raise availability by 6.2 pp pooled (p < 0.001), about 11% of the missing results. Awareness alone has no significant effect on availability but increases explanations. Resources adds nothing beyond Engagement.
- The share of hypotheses left unaccounted for falls by 16.4 pp (about 26% of the control mean). Even so, 44.8% of hypotheses in treated studies still have neither results nor an explanation.
- Study-weighted, the null share is 68.4% among newly released results versus 64.1% at baseline (p = 0.563).
**Beyond prior work:** Franco et al. (2014), Andrews–Kasy (2019) and Brodeur et al. measure study-level or significance-level selection. Abrams et al. (2026) and Leight et al. (2025) audit the registry. This paper measures missingness at the hypothesis level across the full paper and appendix, and adds experimental evidence on what recovers results.
**Surviving claim:** Under-reporting of pre-registered hypotheses in economics is extensive (about 58% missing), much larger than study-level non-publication suggests. Direct, low-cost engagement recovers a modest share.
**Decisive limitations:**
- The claim that null suppression is not the main driver is weaker than the headline. Pooled at the hypothesis level, newly available results are more often null (79.2% vs 68.9%, p = 0.038, footnote 18). The difference disappears only under an alternative baseline classification.
- Hypotheses still missing after the intervention (about 45%) may be selected differently from those recovered.
- Encoding was collaborative rather than independently double-coded; the 10 double-coded studies showed discrepancies.
- The sample is the 2015–17 cohort only.
**Why selected:** It is a consequential metascience contribution with a pre-registered experimental component, and it directly informs registry and journal policy (the Results Report format).

### 4. Jones, Tonetti — Past Automation and Future AI: How Weak Links Tame the Growth Explosion (NBER WP 36050, v1, November 2026)
Link: https://www.nber.org/papers/w36050
**Question:** How much of past US growth came from automation? What does that imply for growth under AI?
**Method:**
- Task model: tasks are gross complements with σ = 0.2 (baseline). In each task, capital and labor are perfect substitutes with their own productivity growth, plus an overall TFP shifter Z.
- Growth accounting uses BEA/BLS production accounts: private business since 1950, plus selected industries (agriculture, computers, motor vehicles, retail, software) over about 40 years.
- The key unobservable, the labor-cost share of tasks newly automated each year, is measured by structured queries to frontier LLMs. The benchmark is the average of GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.7, with 25 draws each; seven LLMs are reported in an appendix.
- Then a calibrated endogenous-automation growth model in which goods and idea production are both automated. Two scenarios: historical patterns, and "Moore's Law everywhere".
**Result:**
- Machines' task productivity grows about 4.9 pp/yr faster than labor's in private business since 1950. Measured automation rates are about 1.8–2.2%/yr (by LLM), around 5%/yr in computers.
- Freezing the set of automated tasks at 1950 would have eliminated essentially all TFP growth: 80–94% of the automation boost is lost across σ ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5} and with automation rates scaled to 0.8×.
- Forward simulation: growth reaches only 4.8% by 2075 in the historical calibration, with a finite-time explosion around 2150. Under "Moore's Law everywhere" the explosion comes around 2050. Weak links (tasks labor still performs) slow the acceleration.
**Beyond prior work:** Aghion–Jones–Jones (2019) and Jones–Liu (2024) are theoretical or back out automation under restrictive assumptions. Caunedo–Keller (2024) assume homogeneous capital productivity. This paper is the first to measure task-level capital and labor productivities empirically and to use them to discipline AI growth scenarios, giving a quantitative counterweight to explosive-growth claims.
**Surviving claim:** Conditional on the measured automation rates and σ well below one, past TFP growth is mostly the result of moving tasks onto fast-improving machines. Weak links make even rapid AI automation a slow path to explosive growth.
**Decisive limitations:**
- The automation rate, the "heroic" measurement in the authors' own words, comes from LLM judgements. There is no validation against task-level ground truth, and variation is larger across models than within them.
- The residual "other TFP" growth is negative for private business at every σ and sharply negative in retail and motor vehicles (Table 8). σ is effectively chosen to keep that residual plausible.
- Forecast dates are scenario outputs, not estimates with uncertainty.
**Why selected:** It is the most consequential macro and growth contribution in the window. It is a first empirical anchor for task-based automation, it speaks directly to the AI growth debate, and its main measurement risk is transparent.

## #S — Scorecards

### NBER w36058 Asker, Collard-Wexler, De Cannière, De Loecker
**Traditions:**
- Empirical IO and market power: Harberger; Bresnahan; De Loecker–Eeckhout–Unger.
- Resource economics: Hotelling; Pindyck; Anderson–Kellogg–Salant.
- Second-best welfare theory: Lipsey–Lancaster; Hsieh–Klenow misallocation.

**Summary:** Comparing observed oil extraction with simulated competitive outcomes on Rystad micro costs gives a naive OPEC welfare cost of $2.2tn since 1970. Removing non-cartel cost-minimization violations cuts it to $0.7tn, almost none of it deadweight loss, so outside frictions amplify cartel power.

**Flags:**
- **Calibration / inference (ME5, ME10): 🟡 fixable / obvious / verified.**
  - The headline totals carry no sampling uncertainty. Demand is estimated from 51 annual observations, and DWL scales roughly 3× across the elasticity range (−0.05 to −0.4).
  - Defense: Table 4 sensitivity is transparent, and IWL is stable at $0.57tn–$0.77tn.
  - Repair: local (bootstrap the demand and cost inputs).
- **Alternatives / attribution (GE5, MI10): 🟠 critical-for-TWL / tricky / acknowledged.**
  - The gap between observed and planner outcomes is attributed to OPEC in a 50-year before-and-after setting (fn. 33).
  - MWL versus IWL partly addresses this, but which frictions are "other" versus cartel-enabled is a modelling choice.
  - Repair: substantial.
- **Economic coherence / welfare (GE7): 🟡 / obvious / acknowledged.** Carbon externalities are excluded, so "welfare loss" means lost gains from trade, not social welfare. The authors flag this and point to a companion paper.
- **Warrant / measurement (MI2): 🟡 / hidden / suspicion.** Productive inefficiency equals cost dispersion in consultancy cost estimates. Unmeasured field-specific costs (political risk, expected cost) would inflate it. The authors acknowledge this in the introduction.
- **Precedence: 🟢.** It extends the authors' 2019 AER paper and cites Stackelberg alternatives.

**Strongest surviving claim:** a sizable share of OPEC's effective market power depends on frictions outside the cartel. Under any parameterization tested, IWL is about one-third of TWL.

### NBER w36054 Walter, Karlan, Moneke, Radu, Zyambo
**Traditions:**
- Design-based applied micro: Angrist–Pischke; Cameron–Gelbach–Miller and MacKinnon–Webb on few-cluster inference.
- Global health epidemiology: HIV treatment interruption (SMART trial lineage) and the HMIS data-quality literature.

**Summary:** USAID-supported Zambian provinces saw deeper and longer HIV-service disruptions than CDC-supported provinces after the 2025 freeze. These were followed by more HIV-positive pregnancies, opportunistic infections and, 10–15 months later, 26% more recorded ART-patient deaths.

**Flags:**

  - Defense: Webb weights, null imposed, randomization-inference joint tests and consistent sign patterns.
  - Repair: local (facility-level clustering with spatial-HAC, or a synthetic control by province).
- **Calibration / multiplicity (ME8): 🟡 / obvious / verified.**
  - Twelve outcomes across three windows are reported without adjustment. The headline death figure is from the final window; the cumulative 15-month CI [−266, 1,665] includes zero.
  - Defense: biological lag ordering (disruption, then infection, then death) is a pre-specified-like logic.
  - Repair: local (family-wise adjustment, or a pre-specified index).
- **Measurement / alternatives (DE5): 🟠 critical / hidden / partially addressed.**
  - USAID paid facility staff, so the freeze could alter recording as well as health. The early dip in recorded deaths is attributed to under-reporting.
  - Defense: section S9 tests missing form sections and placebos (Penta-3, road accidents), and the recorded-death increase arrives after reporting recovered.
  - Repair: substantial (vital-registration or mortuary data).
- **Precedence: 🟢.** It contrasts realized with model-predicted effects (refs 13, 20–24) and acknowledges that the comparison group was also briefly disrupted (a lower bound).

**Strongest surviving claim:** the differential USAID shutdown caused large service disruptions and subsequent increases in HIV-positive pregnancies and opportunistic infections. The mortality estimate is suggestive but window-dependent.

- Field experiments with professionals: List; DellaVigna.
- Research-transparency and registry design: Christensen–Miguel; Olken on pre-analysis plans.

  - Repair: local (bounds on the null share of still-missing hypotheses).

  - Hypothesis encoding was collaborative, not independently double-coded. The double-coded pilot (10 studies) found discrepancies.
  - The denominator of 5,057 hypotheses depends on coding granularity.
  - Repair: local (report inter-coder agreement on a random subsample).

- **Integrity: 🟢.** The authors' own studies are excluded from the sample, and the trial is pre-registered.

**Strongest surviving claim:** hypothesis-level under-reporting in economics is about 58%, far exceeding study-level non-publication. Direct engagement recovers about 11% of missing results.

### NBER w36050 Jones, Tonetti
**Traditions:**
- Growth accounting and semi-endogenous growth: Solow; Jorgenson; Jones (1995).
- Task-based automation: Zeira; Acemoglu–Restrepo; Aghion–Jones–Jones.
- Measurement and construct validity: Groves et al. on survey methodology, here applied to LLM-elicited measures.

**Summary:** Using LLM-measured automation rates in a σ = 0.2 task model, machines' task productivity has outpaced labor's by about 4.9 pp/yr since 1950, and freezing automation in 1950 would have erased nearly all TFP growth. A calibrated endogenous-automation model delays explosive growth to around 2150 (or around 2050 in an extreme scenario) because of weak links.

**Flags:**
- **Warrant / measurement (ME1, construct validity): 🟠 critical / tricky / acknowledged ("heroic").**
  - The automation rate x_t comes from LLM judgements of the share of tasks automated by year. Cross-model dispersion exceeds within-model dispersion, and the paper reports no external validation (e.g., against occupational task data or engineering histories).
  - Defense: freezing results are robust to scaling x_t by 0.8 and to the choice of individual model.
  - Repair: substantial.
- **Economic coherence / calibration (MA1, MA8): 🟡 / tricky / verified.**
  - Residual "other TFP" (Ẑ + ψ̂ℓ) is negative in private business at every σ (≈ −0.1 to −1.9 pp/yr) and strongly negative in retail (−2.0 to −6.1). The authors note that σ above 0.3 gives "implausibly negative" values, so σ is partly identified by plausibility.
  - Repair: local (estimate σ jointly, or report the implied identified set).
- **Calibration (forecasts): 🟡 / obvious / acknowledged.** Explosion dates are speculative scenario outputs. Presenting a range would be better than point dates.
- **Precedence: 🟢.** Positioned clearly against Jones–Liu (2024), Caunedo–Keller (2024) and Young (2025).

**Strongest surviving claim:** under gross complementarity (σ well below 1) and any plausible automation rate, the extensive margin of automation, not better machines on fixed tasks, accounts for most measured TFP growth.
